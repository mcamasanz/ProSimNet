abstract
The disposal and management of dry leaves litter (biomass) from roads and gardens in urban areas is a
challenging task. It is bene ﬁcial to use this waste as an energy source in thermal applications through
pyrolysis and gasi ﬁcation. Downdraft gasi ﬁer is one such promising reactor that can be employed to
achieve this task in a decentralized manner. A distinct pyrolysis zone is established in the gasi ﬁer during
its operation, and it in ﬂuences the performance signi ﬁcantly. Hence, the present work is undertaken to
study the kinetics of pyrolysis in conditions that prevail in gasi ﬁer. A ﬁxed-bed laboratory reactor that
uses biomass pellets is employed to analyze the gases generated during pyrolysis process. Carbon dioxide
and carbon monoxide evolve at relatively lower temperatures while methane and hydrogen form at
higher temperatures. A simpli ﬁed single-step power law kinetic model is used to ﬁt the experimental
data for the gas evolution in ﬁxed bed reactor experiments and the respective kinetic parameters are
estimated. The model thus developed is used successfully to predict the ﬂow rate and the composition of
gas leaving the pyrolysis zone of gasi ﬁer, showing the utility of this approach to obtain an insight into the
gasiﬁer operation.

The renewable energy derived from biomass not only reduces
dependence on fossil fuels but also eliminates the release of excess
carbon dioxide to the atmosphere [ 1]. It also helps in managing
waste generated from agriculture, forests, gardens, food processing
industries, paper, and pulp industries, and aquatic areas, etc. Gar-
den waste forms almost 20% of the municipal solid waste in
metropolitan cities in India [ 2]. The biomass present in the waste
can be converted to solid, liquid, or gaseous fuels to harness its
energy potential. It comprises of cellulose (25 e50 wt %), hemicel-
lulose (15e40%), lignin (10 e40%) along with some amount of ex-
tractives and inorganic content [ 3,4].
There are different thermochemical and biochemical methods
to use bioenergy [ 5]. Pyrolysis is a fundamental thermochemical
process that transforms raw biomass directly into gaseous and
liquid fuels. The literature is replete on the studies in pyrolysis of
woody and non-woody biomass species in the form of agricultural
waste, forest residue, marine waste, etc. It is summarized in Table 1.
Various aspects such as analysis of the volatiles produced, effect of
pyrolysis conditions, and the role of minerals during thermal
heating of biomass, along with kinetic modeling are covered. As can
be seen, a very limited research is performed on garden or agro-
waste owing to the complexities involved. In most of the cases, it
is collected mixed with other wastes (twigs, stems, stumps,
branches, leaves, grass clippings, etc.), and its composition varies
with seasons, and the age of the tree or type of the crop [ 6]. This
waste has a wide range of ash content from 3 to 20% (dry basis).
While pyrolysis as a standalone process has its own application,
it is also an important step in another thermochemical route called
gasiﬁcation wherein, biomass is converted to producer gas
comprising of carbon monoxide and hydrogen. There are various
types of gasi ﬁer designs available depending on the direction of air
and gas ﬂow through the biomass bed, to carry out the gasi ﬁcation
process [ 7]. A novel down-draft gasi ﬁer (see Fig. 1 a) speci ﬁcally
developed for thermal applications (e.g. community cooking), in
our research group runs on high-ash garden waste pellets [ 8]. It
contains a slowly moving bed of biomass pellets, and air is sent
midway along the height of the gasi ﬁer. Biomass pellets are ignited
with torch ignition, which results in a development of the com-
bustion zone around the port where air is introduced. The heat is
transferred above and below the combustion zone via convection,

The present work aims at development of a working kinetic
model for pyrolysis of garden waste pellets and the estimation of
associated kinetic parameters. It may be noted that the model is
developed speci ﬁcally in context of downdraft gasi ﬁer. It can help
predict the amount and composition of the gas supplied by the
pyrolysis zone to the combustion zone thereby making the overall
reactor modelling and simulation exercise a lot easier. It may be
noted that the intra-pellet transport limitations interfere with the
intrinsic reaction kinetics, and that the model developed here is a
working kinetic model applicable in the conditions mentioned.
Nonetheless, the work presents an approach towards accounting
for pyrolysis in reactor model, which can be extended easily to
different biomass feeds in downdraft gasi ﬁer.
2. Materials and method
The lignocellulosic garden waste was collected from roadside,
IIT-B, Mumbai campus. The leaves collected from different trees
were washed thoroughly to remove the upper layer of soil and then
dried in an oven. These dried leaves were milled and sieved to
obtain a particle size in the range of 90 e150 mm as the further
reduction in size is dif ﬁcult by normal milling practices. The feed-
stock was characterized in terms of proximate analysis, ultimate
analysis, and chemical composition, i.e., cellulose, hemicellulose,
and lignin [ 3]. The results are given in Table 2. The higher heating
values of biomass species were determined using Bomb Calorim-
eter (IKA C-200) and they were found to be in the range 16 e22 kJ/
mol, which is greater than that of wood. The characterization of the
lignocellulosic biomass conﬁrms its potential as a source of energy.
The volatile content of this feedstock is in the range of 65 e74% that
makes it more promising for pyrolysis study [ 3].
2.1. Preliminary experiments in downdraft gasifer
The operating windows for temperature and heating rate that
prevail in the gasi ﬁer were determined by conducting selected
experiments. The temperatures at different locations were
measured and recorded during its operation. A running gasi ﬁer
with 40 kg pellets and air ﬂowing at a ﬂow rate of 23 kg/h, was
quenched in the middle of its operation, and samples at different
locations were removed to estimate the extent of pyrolysis along
the gasiﬁer height. The amount of volatile content was observed to
be varying along the height of the gasi ﬁer as shown in Fig. 1c. The
variation in volatile content shows that pyrolysis occurs throughout
the gasi ﬁer at varying temperatures. However, it can be seen that
almost 60e65% of pyrolysis takes place above the combustion zone.
This zone is a source of combustible gases to the combustion zone,
and that the composition and ﬂow rate of these gases have a great
inﬂuence on the temperature of the combustion zone and hence, on
the performance of the gasi ﬁer. Its realistic prediction is important
and the present work is aimed at serving this purpose.
The temperature was measured in the gasi ﬁer during the
experiment and variations were observed along the length [ 8]. The
rate of change in the surrounding gas temperature relative to pel-
lets can be calculated based on the temperature pro ﬁle and the
pellet residence time. A sample calculation is shown in Appendix -
A. The temperature variation rate varies over the range 17 e24 K/
min in the pyrolysis zone. We have chosen heating rate (20 K/min)
in this range, while generating the data in the laboratory ﬁxed bed
reactor.
2.2. Kinetic studies in ﬁxed bed reactor
To determine the composition of volatiles containing tar and
pyrolysis gases such as CO 2,C O ,C H 4, and H 2,a ﬁxed bed reactor
setup was used, which could accommodate relatively large amount
of biomass (see Fig. 2 ). Furthermore, it is geometrically similar to
the downdraft gasiﬁer. The biomass in the pellet form was used for
conducting experiments in the ﬁxed bed reactor. To prepare pellets,
garden waste was ﬁrst shredded and then manually fed to the
pelletizer to produce pellets of 15 mm diameter and 50 mm length.
The detailed process for pelletization and different steps required
to prepare pellets are discussed in our earlier work [ 21]. The ﬁxed
bed reactor setup comprises of a stainless steel (SS) reactor of outer
diameter 5 cm and length 30 cm. The reaction can be performed at
different heating rates (5 e30 K/min) using a programmable elec-
trical furnace. The reactor environment was maintained in a ni-
trogen atmosphere, at a ﬂow rate of 500 ml/min throughout the
experiment. The volume and amount of resultant gases and tar
formed, respectively, were determined with respect to time and
temperature. The reactor temperature was measured using ther-
mocouple placed at the centre of the reactor. A two-stage ice jac-
keted condenser was used to separate the tar and the gases
produced in the reactor. An acetone impinger was used at the exit
of the reactor to ef ﬁciently collect the tar produced. The biomass
pellets weighing ~60 g for different species were placed in the ﬁxed
bed reactor and heated at a rate of 20 K/min, and the pyrolysis gases
produced were analysed using GC-TCD. The condensable volatiles
were collected in an ice jacketed tar collector. The identi ﬁed non-
condensable gases were CO 2,C H 4, CO, and H 2. The effects of tem-
perature and time on tar formation were studied. The light hy-
drocarbons were collected in bladders and analysed using GC-TCD,
and the residue char was further used for gasi ﬁcation studies. The
tar collected was characterized in terms of ultimate analysis as
shown in Table 1. The calori ﬁc values of both tar and biomass fuel
were estimated using the equations given by Demirbas (2017) [ 22],
and, Channiwala and Parikh (2002) [ 23], and also by a bomb calo-
rimeter. The HHV values estimated by the two methods were found
to be close with the difference of less than 10%. As explained later,
the data for gas evolution obtained from the above experiments
was ﬁtted using the Arrhenius model (Single Reaction Model) to
estimate the kinetic parameters.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Gas evolution in FBR
Fig. 3 shows the gas evolution pro ﬁle with a change in tem-
perature for biomass pyrolysis at 20 K/min. It is clear that the for-
mation of CO 2, and CO starts early in the temperature range of
600e800 K. CO 2 evolution is related to cracking of functional
groups such as C ]O (carbonyl), and COOH [ 24e26]. CO gas is
evolved due to cracking of groups like C eOeC (carboxyl) and C ]O
(carbonyl) present majorly in cellulose and hemicellulose [ 26]. The
two peaks for CO formation are observed in the temperature range
of 650e800 K and above 900 K. The ﬁrst peak is associated with the
decomposition of C eC and C eO bonds originated from cellulose
and hemicellulose, while the peak at higher temperatures is due to
the decomposition of lignin. The formation of methane is associ-
ated with cracking of methoxy group (O eCH3), which is majorly
present in aromatic part of lignin [24,25]. The methane formation is
directly associated with the lignin content in biomass. The
hydrogen formation starts at a relatively high temperature
(>700 K), with the maximum formation in the temperature range
of 930 e1000 K. The formation of hydrogen is associated with
cracking of CeH, and C ]C bonds present in aromatic groups and is
also due to cracking of heavy volatiles at high temperatures [ 27,28].
A detailed description on the breakage of several organic com-
pounds present in biomass is discussed in Appendix B. The exper-
iments were conducted until hydrogen gas formation ceases. The
amount of tar produced during the pyrolysis process at 20 K/min
was in the range of 190 e260 g/m 3 of the total gas produced for
these biomasses. The calori ﬁc value of tar was estimated using the
data on ultimate analysis (Channiwala and Parikh (2002) [ 23]), and
also by bomb calorimeter. The calori ﬁc values of the tars produced
from all the biomass species were found to be in the range,
31e35 MJ/kg. While the extent of tar formation varied from
biomass to biomass, interestingly, the composition of non-

are higher as compared to other two species having higher ash
contents. The residue left at the end is considered as char, and the
more the ash content the more is the char formation [ 30,32].

3.3. Check for transport limitations
As mentioned before, the main reason for employing the
simpliﬁed kinetics is to avoid multiscale and rigorous modelling
associated with transport limitations. The heat transfer resistance
affects the rate of reaction with the increase in particle size and
heating rate. In this section, we provide evidence of such transport
limitation by performing experiments in TGA on powdered biomass
and a single pellet, separately. The data thus obtained is further
compared with the kinetics obtained in our laboratory ﬁxed bed
reactor. The results are shown in Fig. 5 . The TGA runs (powdered
sample) represent intrinsic kinetics. The pyrolysis kinetics for the
pellets, both in TGA and that in the ﬁxed bed reactor, were observed
to be slower than the intrinsic kinetics. The difference between the
rates of reaction on powder and pellet indicated the presence of
strong heat transport limitation in case of reactions with pellets.
The slight difference between the rates of pellet reactions in TGA
and that in the ﬁxed bed reactor (FBR) may be attributed to the
external heat transport limitation in the FBR. While the TGA results
were found to be independent of heating rate, the transport limi-
tations in ﬁxed bed reactor can also be realised by varying the
heating rate.
When the experiments were conducted at different heating
rates in the ﬁxed bed reactor using raintree pellets, the variations in
the yield of char, tar and gases were observed. With the increase in
heating rate, char yield decreased, while tar and non-condensable
gases increased. When the heating rates of 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 K/min
were employed to pyrolyze the raintree pellets, the char produced
was 33%, 30%, 29%, 27%, and 25% respectively. In the same way, the
amounts of tar increased from 15% to 39% when heating rate was
increased from 5 to 30 K/min. At slower heating rate, volatiles
produced spent longer time in the reactor, which resulted into
secondary reactions. The secondary reactions include further
cracking, re-polymerization, re-condensation, that result into char
formation and cracking of heavy volatiles [ 36].
With an increase in the heating rate, the heat transfer from the
hot reactor wall to the pellets starts showing up its effect. Owing to

3.4. Validation of the kinetic model
The working kinetic model developed in the earlier section is
used here to explain the results of the reactor runs. Fig. 7 shows the
compartment model of the gasi ﬁer which is divided in three
different zones - pyrolysis, combustion and gasi ﬁcation based on
the dominating reaction in the respective zones. The objective is to
estimate the ﬂow rate and composition of the stream leaving the
pyrolysis zone which acts as a source of mass and heat to the
combustion zone. For modelling purpose, the pyrolysis zone is
assumed to be a cascade of back-mixed reactors with co-current
ﬂow of biomass and the product gas. The model for every reactor
in the cascade is also given in Fig. 7. It involves material balances on
both solid and gas sides. The energy balance is not considered here,
and the temperature pro ﬁle is directly adopted from the experi-
ments (see Fig. 1). Hence, it must be noted that the model given in
Fig. 7 is not a complete model in a true sense, and it is used here
only to demonstrate the applicability of the kinetics developed.
TheEquations (5e7) in Fig. 7 are solved using MATLAB nonlinear
algebraic equation solver to obtain the pro ﬁle of volatile content in
the pyrolysis zone along the height of the gasi ﬁer, and to calculate
the ﬂow rate, composition and calori ﬁc value of the gas stream
(stream q in Fig. 7) that acts as a source to the combustion zone. The
pyrolysis zone was divided into suf ﬁciently large number (16) of
equal sections, each of height 2 cm, and each section is considered
as a completely back-mixed reactor of uniform composition. The
volatile content (wt. %) predicted at different locations are plotted

4. Conclusion
The pyrolysis of ligno-cellulosic garden waste results in release
of CO 2 and CO ﬁrst due to the decomposition of cellulose and
hemicellulose. At temperatures > 673 K, hydrogen and methane are
evolved due to the presence of aromatic and methoxy group in
lignin. Tar formation was monitored with time and temperature,
and the amount of tar produced increases with increase in heating
rate. While the compositions of non-condensables are almost
similar for all the species, the extents tar produced from eucalyptus
and raintree were found to be greater than that from the other two
species. The kinetics for the formation of non-condensable gases
and tar can be well explained by nth order single-step reaction
model that assumes direct conversion of biomass to gases. The
values of activation energy for each gas evolution reaction were
found to be dependent on the heating rate which indicates that the
heat and mass transport limitations at particle level play a crucial
role in the kinetics.
Furthermore, the utility of this apparent pyrolysis kinetics is
demonstrated to predict the ﬂow rate and the composition of the
gas leaving the pyrolysis zone. The predicted results match
reasonably well with the experimental ﬁndings. This, we believe, is
an important step in the development of an exhaustive compart-
ment model of downdraft gasi ﬁer.

Appendix B
FTIR.
The FTIR analysis of the raintree char prepared at different
temperatures is shown in Figure B1 . The char was prepared at
different temperatures ranging from 473 to 1 173 K, and analysed
using FTIR to study the change in chemical composition/functional
group with increasing temperature. Scanning was carried out in the
wavelength range of 400 e4000 cm /C0 1 with the step size of
4c m/C0 1 at a scanning rate of 40. The evidence of bond breakage as
represented by a change in IR peak heights was validated by
observing gas composition at these temperatures. The change in
functional group of char produced on heating biomass at different
temperatures would help in explaining the pyrolysis mechanism.
The FTIR spectra of raw biomass (dry leaves sample at 303 K) shows
the complex transmittance bands, with the various number of
functional groups present. At the temperature of 1023 K, biomass
gets fully decomposed to char, and there are very little organic
functional groups left in the char.
Figure B1 shows that peaks in the range of 3200 e3600 cm /C0 1
that correspond to the O - H group [26], and this peak is observed in
all the four biomass species. The O eH peak is also associated with
glucoside linkages of cellulose, or hydroxyphenyl, guaiacyl, and
syringyl group of lignin [ 39]. The decomposition of cellulose and
lignin is related to breakage of OeH bond. The bands in the range of
2900e3000 cm /C0 1 are usually associated with the aliphatic C eH
groups [ 40]. In all the biomass species, it was observed that on
heating, C eH bond peak disappeared after 700 K. This disappear-
ance is associated with the breaking of alkyl groups present, and it
results in the formation of methane, ethane and ethylene [ 41]. In
raintree, eucalyptus, and mango, there is a band in the region
around 1730 cm/C0 1, which indicates the presence of C]O (aldehyde/
ketone) which are related to a free carbonyl compound in the
hemicellulose [ 42]. All the four biomass species have peaks near
1629 cm/C0 1 which is associated with C ]C of aromatic compounds
as mentioned in Yang et al. (2007) [26]. This peak disappeared in
the char produced at a temperature greater than 1000 K. The gas
release with an increase in temperature results into the disap-
pearance of various functional groups. The peak in the range
1470e1430 cm /C0 1 is related to the presence of methoxy group
(OeCH3), which results in the release of methane gas at a higher
temperature ( >700 K) [ 26]. The peak at around ~1 158 cm /C0 1 was
found for eucalyptus biomass and is related to the C eOeC bond
which shows the presence of cellulose and hemicellulose [ 43]. The
peak in the range ~1053 cm /C0 1 was observed in raintree and euca-
lyptus at 303 K, which is associated with the presence of C ]O, C-3
and CeC bonds which are attributed to cellulose content [ 42]. The
decomposition of cellulose was also seen after 800 K because the
peak got diminished after heating biomass at > 800 K. The FTIR
peak in the range of 700 e900 cm /C0 1 corresponds to the CeH group
of aromatic compounds [ 26] and was found in all the four biomass
species. This peak was not observed in the FTIR spectra at room
temperature i.e. at 303 K. This peaks were observed on heating
biomass char from 523 K to 653 K but later started to diminish after
653 K in all the cases. This appearance of the peak in a particular
temperature range is associated with increasing aromaticity which
gets broken down on further heating, is con ﬁrmed by the disap-
pearance of peak at a higher temperature ( >1 100 K). Reduction of
these bands at higher temperature shows the carbonization due to
cracking and rebuilding of aromatic rings, which result in the
release of hydrogen gas [ 41].
